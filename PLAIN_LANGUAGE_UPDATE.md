# Our brain project, in plain words — where it stands (16 Nov 2026)

**What we set out to do.** Compare two brains using real scans and real measurements: a
newborn human baby's brain, and an adult lion's brain. Not stories about them — numbers.

**The strange discovery.** The lion's scan existed in a public archive, but with no labels
saying which part is which. Then we found that the team who made the scans had drawn those
labels years ago — they were sitting on the project's own website, never put into the
archive, and nobody had ever used them. So we took the whole set: 37 animals, from a mouse
to a gorilla to a dolphin.

**The checking.** Before using any of it, we checked every label by eye against its scan.
About half the files turned out to be empty — right name, nothing inside. One file named
"hippocampus" was actually a whole brain. We threw all of those out, in writing. What
survived is a smaller set we can stand behind.

**What we found.**
- The lion's hippocampus — the part of the brain that builds maps and memories — had never
  been measured. It is 2.02 cubic centimetres, about 1% of its brain.
- Across the ten animals whose labels passed checking, one clear pattern: the bigger the
  brain, the smaller the hippocampus's *share* of it. A ferret gives it nearly 3% of its
  brain; a giraffe, about half of one percent.
- The baby result, which two different methods agree on: a newborn human's hippocampus is
  almost exactly the size that pattern predicts for *any* mammal with a brain that size.
  And a newborn's whole brain shape is as different from an adult human's as it is from a
  lion's. In plain words: at birth, we are mammals first. Whatever makes the human brain
  human is mostly built after birth, not before.
- We also measured the newborns' wiring — the 54 main cables that connect the brain —
  region by region, for the same ten babies.

**A tool came out of it.** Finding the labels was not luck; we turned the method into a
small program ("Goldmine Hunter") that checks whether a project's public archive is
missing files its own website has. Run cold, it found the lion labels again on its own.

**Where it stands now.** We are filling in the animals whose labels were empty — the
gorilla, the chimpanzee, the wolf, and the adult human in the collection — by carefully
fitting outlines from reference brains of close relatives, checking each one by eye, and
throwing out any that don't fit honestly. (The adult human piece is briefly stuck on a
download problem with the reference site; we are fetching it another way.) After that,
the plan is to look for a deeper rule in the numbers, write our prediction down *before*
testing it, and then test it against measurements other scientists published long ago —
so the test is fair and we can't move the goalposts.

Nothing has been published or posted publicly yet. Everything is saved, checked, and
reproducible: anyone with the files can re-run our work and get the same numbers.
